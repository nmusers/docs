* Population models
In this chapter we discuss models for populations data. Those data
contain observations from a set of individuals sampled from a
population. Such data are characterized by one or more
observations for each individual. All the models
discussed here are /parameter models/, in that observations are described
with models with parameters. These models are capable of explaining the
data variability due to the those unknown random parameters.

** Problem formulation
Individuals in a population of size \(N\) are different due to
/fixed/ and/or /random/ effects, manifested
as individual parameters \(\phi_i\), \((i=1,\dots,N)\), with
individual ID \(i\) in subscript. Then a general formulation for /mixed-effects/ model is to use certain
function \(f\) of known quantities
\(x_{ij}\) and unknown parameters \(\phi_i\) to describe the
observations \(y_{ij}\). This function \(f()\) is sometimes refered as
/structure model/. In reality \(f(x_{ij}, \phi_i)\) does not
account for all the variability of \(y_{ij}\), therefore we additively
complement it with a /residual error/ term \(\epsilon_{ij}\). In summary

$$
y_{ij} = f(x_{ij}, \phi_i) + \epsilon_{ij}.
$$

Note
- Subscript \(ij\), \(j=1\dots,N_i\) denotes observation \(j\) for
  individual \(i\). Individuals often do not have same number of
  observations. That is, in general \(N_i\ne N_k\) when \(i\ne k\).
  The total number of observations in a data set is \(N_{\text{obs}}=\sum_iN_i\).

- The term "nonlinear" in "nonlinear mixed-effects model" refers the
  fact that \(f()\) is often nonlinear with respect to \(x_{ij}\)
  and/or \(\phi_i\).

- NONMEM assumes that \(\epsilon\), as a vector containing all
  \(\epsilton_{ij}\) for all the \(i\)'s and \(j\)'s, follows a multivariate normal
  distribution [fn:3],
  $$
  \epsilon \sim \text{MultiNormal}(0, \Sigma).
  $$
  NONMEM uses reserved variable =SIGMA= for the covariance matrix
  \(\Sigma\). When \(\epsilon_{ij}\) are independent from each other,
  \(\Sigma\) degenerates into a single value \(\sigma^2\). It describes
  the residual error variance of all the observations.

Next we describe \(\phi_i\) using another model, by /assuming/each \(\phi_i\) deviates a
"typical" value by a random perturbation \(\eta_i\). By "typical" we mean
that the value is the same for individuals with same covariates
\(z_{i}\), e.g. weight and height. This another model is described by
function \(\mu()\). That is, \(\mu(z_i, \theta)\), with parameter
\(\theta\) and covariate \(z_i\) gives the "typical" value of \(\phi_i\). In summary

$$
\phi_{i} = \mu(z_{i}, \theta) + \eta_{i}.
$$

Note
- We refer \(z_i\) as /fixed effects/, and \(\theta\) /fixed effect
  parameters/ [fn:1]. NONMEM uses reserved variable =THETA= for
  \(\theta\) [fn:2].
- We refer \(\eta_i\) as /random effects/. NONMEM uses reserved
  variable =ETA= for \(\eta\).
- A nonlinear \(\mu()\) contributes to additional nonliearity of the problem.
- In general for each individual, \(\theta\), \(\phi\), and \(\eta\) are vectors.
- NONMEM assumes all \(\eta_i\)'s follow a multivariate normal
  distribution [fn:3],
  $$
  \eta_i \sim \text{MultiNormal}(0, \Omega).
  $$
  NONMEM uses reserved variable =OMEGA= for the covariance matrix \(\Omega\).
- NONMEM output use =PHI= for \(\phi_i\)'s.
- In some of NONMEM's solution algorithms the above \(\mu() + \eta\) pattern can improve
  efficiency. See @@html:<a href="/user-guide/techniques/mu_model/">MU referencing</a>@@.

** Structure parameter models
Now let us examine a few common models for \(\mu()\). Assume \(\phi\)
has clearance CL as one of its elements,
then we use \(\tilde{\text{CL}_i}=\mu(z_i, \theta)\) for =TVCL= [fn:4], the "typical
value" of CL.

*** Linear (additive) models
The simplest form of \(\mu()\) is its being linearly related to
\(\theta\). For example, if CL is
the sum of renal and non-renal components (CL_{ren} and CL_{met}, respectively),
and CL_{ren} is
believed to be proportional to renal function (RF) as described according
to a standard formula involving covariates in \(z\): age (AGE),
serum creatinine (SECR), and weight (WT), then one might consider the
following model (we omit subscript \(i\) to avoid clutter).

$$
\tilde{\text{CL}}_{\text{met}} = \theta_1 \text{WT},
$$
$$
\text{RF}=\text{WT}\frac{1.66-0.011\text{AGE}}{\text{SECR}},
$$
$$
\tilde{\text{CL}}_{\text{ren}} = \theta_4 \text{RF},
$$
$$
\tilde{\text{CL}} = \tilde{\text{CL}}_{\text{ren}} + \tilde{\text{CL}}_{\text{met}}.
$$
Despite RF being a nonlinear function of covariates, the above model
shows a linear \(\mu\) with respect to \(\theta\), as (we use \(\cdot\) for inner product)
$$
\tilde{\text{CL}} = (\text{WT}, \text{RF}) \cdot (\theta_1, \theta_4).
$$
*** Multiplicative model
Multiplicative models are linear models on a logarithmic scale.
For example, if patients covering a very wide range of weights
are studied, metabolic clearance might vary with weight, but
not linearly. Then instead of modeling them linearly, we assume
$$
\log(\tilde{\text{CL}}_{\text{met}}) = \theta_1 + \theta_2\log(\text{WT}),
$$
equivalently [fn:5]
$$
\tilde{\text{CL}}_{\text{met}} = \theta_1\text{WT}^{\theta_2}.
$$
Note that the two sets of \(\theta\)'s are not the same, but since
they are unknown parameters we can transform them and fit either set
in a model. This /reparameterization/ is common in statistical modeling.
*** Saturation Models
A useful model for processes reaching a maximum is a hyperbolic model.
For example, if a second drug, (whose steady-state plasma
concentration, Cpss_2 is known and given in the data set), is present
in some individuals and it is believed to be an inhibitor of the
metabolism of the study drug, one might consider

$$
\tilde{\text{CL}}_{\text{met}}=\text{WT} \left(\theta_1-\frac{\theta_2 \text{Cpss}_2}{\theta_3 + \text{Cpss}_2}\right)
$$

The relationshiup is shown in figure below.  The inhibition is expressed by the
ratio occurring within the brackets and is a concave hyperbola,
asymptoting
to a maximum value equal to \(\theta_2\).  It is identical in form to
the Michaelis-Menten model.

*** Indicator variables
Assume a binary variable HF documents the clinical condition of heart
failure as "present" (HF=0) or "absent" (HF=1). To model heart failure
affected CL_{met}, we do [fn:5]
$$
\tilde{\text{CL}}_\text{met} = (\theta_1 - \theta_2\text{HF})\text{WT}.
$$
See details in @@html:<a href="/user-guide/techniques/indi/"> indicator variable in modeling techniques</a>@@.

** Population random effects models
Under NONMEM nomenclature, there are two levels of random effects,
\(\eta\) and \(\epsilon\):

- \(\eta\) in the parameter model accounts for the /inter-individual/
  variability in \(\phi\) that is not explained by \(\mu(z_i, \theta)\);
- \(\epsilon\) accounts for the /intra-individual/ variability that is
  not explained by \(f(x_{ij}, \phi_i)\), thus formulates the error model.

The interindividual variability can arise from
- the approximate nature of \(\mu()\) to express the relation between
  covariates \(z\) and individual parameters \(\phi\);
- the covariates \(z\) contain measurement error.

The intraindividual variability can arise from
- the approximate nature of the structure model \(\f()\);
- observed quantities \(x\) contain measurement error;
- the pharmacokinetic responses \(y\) contain measurement error (assay error).

*** Models for Interindividual variability
NONMEM uses \(\eta\) to describe interindividual variability.
Its covariance matrix \(\Omega\) is symmetric and
positive-semidefinite. NONMEM utilizes the symmetry so that only the
/lower triangular elements/ are used to specifiy =OMEGA=. For example,
in a two-compartment model we can have \(\eta=(\eta_{k10}, \eta_{k12},
\eta_{k21})\) for the elimination and transfer coefficients. Then
\(\Omega\) will be a 3x3 matrix but =OMEGA= contains only 6 elements:
(\(\text{cov}(\cdot,\cdot)\) indicates covariance).

$$
\omega_{11}=\text{cov}(\eta_{k10}, \eta_{k10});\\
\omega_{21}=\text{cov}(\eta_{k10}, \eta_{k12});\\
\omega_{31}=\text{cov}(\eta_{k10}, \eta_{k21});\\
\omega_{22}=\text{cov}(\eta_{k12}, \eta_{k12});\\
\omega_{23}=\text{cov}(\eta_{k12}, \eta_{k21});\\
\omega_{33}=\text{cov}(\eta_{k21}, \eta_{k21}).
$$

**** Additive model
An additive model, for example for volumn of distribution V:

$$
\text{V}=\tilde{\text{V}}+\eta
$$

leads to unit-dependent \(\omega\), as changing the unit of V will
cause \(\eta\) to change, so does its variance.

**** Multiplicative model
Also known as Constant Coefficient of Variation (CCV) model or
proportional error model, a multiplicative model such as
$$
\text{V}=\tilde{\text{V}}(1+\eta)
$$
does not change \(\omega\) when unit of V is alterered.
